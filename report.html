Forex Backtest Postmortem

Eleven thousand strategy configurations and three and a half thousand statistical tests, run against a $200 account. None of them made money — and the three that looked like they did are the most useful part of the record.

Instruments 10 FX pairs, 1-minute Period Jan 2024 – Aug 2026 Source Dukascopy bid/ask Sizing 0.01 lot, $200

No strategy tested produced a profit that survived out-of-sample validation.

That includes the original EMA-crossover idea, three alternative indicator families, a cross-sectional currency portfolio, and a two-leg statistical-arbitrage book. The failure is consistent across two independent date ranges and ten instruments, which makes it a property of the approach rather than of one unlucky sample.

Every configuration was fitted on Jan 2024 – Aug 2025 and scored on Sep 2025 – Aug 2026, a twelve-month holdout that was never used for selection.

The original strategy

EMA 5/13 crossover, stop at 1.5×ATR, targets at 1R/2R/3R. Tuned across fast and slow periods, stop multipliers and reward ratios, on four pairs at M5 and M15.

Only two configurations cleared breakeven in-sample and both collapsed on the holdout. The problem is structural rather than a matter of settings: an unfiltered crossover fires roughly sixteen times a day, and at 0.01 lots each round trip costs about 1.5–2 pips in spread, slippage and commission. The EURUSD M5 variant paid that toll 8,287 times.

Why win rate was the wrong target

A 66% win rate that lost 159% of the account

A Bollinger reversion setup on GBPUSD M5 with a half-R target won 69.0% of trades in-sample and 66.3% on the holdout — clearing the original 65% goal on both windows — while returning −182% and −159%.

It won two trades out of three taking about five pips, then gave it all back on one fifteen-pip loss. Win rate is purchasable at will by shrinking the target, which is why selection here ran on profit factor instead.

Three results that looked real

Each of these passed the checks in place at the time. Each would have shipped a strategy that backtested beautifully and lost money live. Finding them is what the framework is for.

Drift confound

A 24-hour reversal effect

All four pairs agreed on the sign, the weakest t-statistic was 2.3, and the effect held at +1.88 pips in-sample and +1.77 on the holdout. It was measuring raw forward returns, so it had absorbed the market's own drift. Subtracting each window's baseline left 0.5–1.5 pips — under the cost of trading it — and USDJPY reversed sign on the holdout.

Overlapping windows

Daily time-series momentum

Trailing 20–60 day momentum showed t-statistics up to 4.05 across three of four pairs — a publishable-looking result. Consecutive observations overlapped by 95%, inflating the effective sample. Recomputed on non-overlapping holding periods, nothing reached significance, and AUDUSD ran opposite in sign to the rest.

Implementation bug

A hedge that wasn't hedged

The pairs book produced the best numbers of the project — holdout profit factors above 3 and 4. Every top result paired against USDJPY. Shorting a spread A − βB means −1 of A and +β of B, so a negative beta requires both legs short; the code hardcoded opposite legs. For those combinations there was no hedge at all — just a doubled directional bet riding the 2024–26 USDJPY trend while appearing market-neutral.

Judge the distribution, not the leaderboard

The leaderboard for this run showed holdout profit factors of 3.17 and 3.15 — and they are noise. With 1,362 configurations tested, a few spectacular results are what randomness delivers on samples that small. The distribution is the honest summary: it sits almost entirely below breakeven.

The same logic gives a cleaner test than any single number. If a strategy has no edge, the share of configurations profitable in both windows should land near the in-sample share times the roughly even odds of a positive holdout. Here 15.9% were profitable in-sample, predicting about 8% — and 2.7% delivered. Below chance, which is what paying costs on two legs with no edge looks like.

Why more tuning would not have helped

The best configuration still loses with spread, slippage and commission all set to zero — profit factors of 0.80 to 0.95 across every pair. It is not the broker, the costs, or the position sizing. There is no predictive signal in the entry.

That result generalizes further than it first appears. Under zero conditional drift, every arrangement of stops and targets has zero expectancy before costs — so once the scans found no drift to exploit, the failure of eleven thousand stop/target combinations followed necessarily. It was not bad luck in the search.

The broader context is consistent with this. Brokers are required to publish their retail outcomes, and the figure sits at 70–80% of accounts losing money. Major currency pairs are the most heavily arbitraged market in the world; simple indicator patterns in them were competed away long ago. These results are the expected outcome, reproduced carefully.

What holds up

  1. Do not fund a live account with any strategy tested here. The original EMA crossover would have lost the $200 stake, and the record now shows exactly how.
  2. The testing framework is the durable asset. It resolves every trade on 1-minute data so stop-versus-target ordering comes from the data rather than an assumption, charges real spreads, fills at the next bar's open, and enforces a holdout. Any future idea can be put through it in minutes.
  3. Check the three failure modes on anything new. Demean against the period's drift, use non-overlapping windows for significance, and confirm a hedge is actually hedging. Each one produced a convincing false positive in this project.
  4. If the hunt continues, look at structure rather than indicators. Scheduled news reactions and less-arbitraged instruments are where a retail-accessible edge would plausibly still live. Machine learning on these same features is the fastest route to a spectacular backtest and a near-certain false positive.
